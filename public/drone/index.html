    §4 Drone swarming background · §6 Levels of autonomy · §7 Networks

    From melee to swarm

    Group fighting has evolved in four steps. Each step adds coordination; swarming adds it without a commander.

    Progression of combat (Fig. 4.1)

    Swarm vs. single entity (§4.3, §2)

    8 2.2
    • A ship's weapons engage one target at a time.
      • Analogy: one goalkeeper facing eight penalty shots taken at once.
    • Every extra drone adds a target; the ship's kill rate stays fixed.
      • Attack from every bearing at once = no safe side to face.
    • NPS simulated 8 IED drones vs. an Arleigh Burke destroyer (Pham 2012):
      • 3.82 drones hit with current defenses.
      • 1.12 with their improved design.
    • Small drones also have small radar signatures, so detection is late and the engagement window short.
    • The report's thesis: answer a swarm with a counter-swarm (§4.4), not a bigger gun.

    Toy model: the ship detects at 160 m, then needs the chosen seconds per kill. Drones fly at 22 m/s from 300 m. Raise the attacker count and watch the leak rate climb.

    Levels of autonomy (§6)

    Network types (§7, Fig. 7.1)

    Click a node to destroy it
    • Centralized: one command node; fast to it, slow between agents. Hit the hub and everyone goes deaf.
    • Decentralized: several hubs, each with a subgroup. A hit costs one subgroup.
    • Distributed: no hub; each agent talks only to neighbours (like the Princeton army-ant bridges, §4.2). Any agent can die with no loss to the rest.
      • This is why the RL agent in §11.1.1 gives every drone its own copy of one network.
    §5 Reinforcement learning background

    How an agent learns from reward

    Four ideas, each built on the last: the MDP, the Bellman value, Q-learning, and policy optimization (PPO, SAC). Same gridworld as the report's Figures 5.1 to 5.6.

    0.90 0.20

    1 · MDP and the Bellman equation (§5.1)

    V(s) = maxa [ R(s,a) + γ Σs′ T(s,a,s′) V(s′) ]
    • State s: a snapshot of the world (here: which square).
    • Action a: move up / down / left / right.
    • Transition T(s,a,s′): the chance an action lands where intended. "Slip chance" sends you sideways.
    • Reward R: +1 at the goal; −1 at a hazard.
    • Value V(s): reward reachable from here, discounted by γ per step.
      • Analogy: heat spreading out from a radiator. Each sweep lets value flow one more square away from the goal.
      • Lower γ = value fades faster with distance: a short-sighted agent.

    Click any square to see its Bellman backup: each action's expected value, and the max that wins.

    Click a square.

    2 · Q-learning (§5.2, §5.2.1)

    0.30 0.50
    Q(s,a) ← Q(s,a) + α [ r + γ maxa′ Q(s′,a′) − Q(s,a) ]
    • The agent starts knowing nothing: every square white (Fig. 5.2).
    • It wanders, and each move nudges one Q(s,a) toward "reward now + best value next".
      • Each square shows four triangles: one Q value per action.
      • Value seeps backward from the goal one visit at a time (Figs. 5.3, 5.4).
    • No transition model is needed: sampling replaces T(s,a,s′) (Eq. 2 → Eq. 3).
    • ε trades exploration for exploitation: random move with chance ε.
    • Why it fails for drones: a drone battle's state is continuous: 10 friends and 10 foes in 3-D. No table can hold it.

    Chart: steps per episode. It falls as the agent learns the short way to the goal.

    3 · Policy gradient, actor-critic (§5.2.2, Fig. 5.6)

    0.40
    θ ← θ + α · Q̂(s,a) ∇πθ(a|s) / πθ(a|s)
    • Skip values; learn the policy πθ(a|s) directly: a probability for each action.
      • Bars in each square = the four action probabilities (Fig. 5.6). They start random.
    • The critic predicts how good the state is. After each move:
      • error δ = what happened − what the critic expected.
      • δ > 0: better than expected → raise that action's bar.
      • δ < 0: worse → lower it.
    • Works with continuous actions, which is why the drone agent uses it.
    • Weakness: noisy gradients, and one oversized step can wreck a good policy. PPO fixes that next.

    4a · PPO: clip the step (§5.3)

    0.30

    x-axis: ratio r = πnew(a|s) / πold(a|s). The objective min(rA, clip(r, 1−ε, 1+ε)A) goes flat once the new policy has moved far enough, so the gradient is zero and the update stops pushing. The report's config uses ε = 0.3.

    4b · Why the clip matters

    0.60

    Task: pick a number a; reward peaks at a = 0.6. Each learner samples 32 actions, then reuses that batch for 10 gradient steps. Unclipped (red) overshoots and thrashes; PPO (blue) stops each update inside its trust region and settles.

    4c · SAC: reward randomness (§5.3)

    0.60
    • PPO (on-policy): learn only from the current policy's own fresh experience; cap each step.
      • Analogy: a cautious driver who never turns the wheel more than a few degrees per second.
    • SAC (off-policy): reuse old experience and add an entropy bonus so the policy stays curious.
      • π(a) ∝ exp(Q(a)/α). High α → flat, exploratory; low α → greedy.
    • Unity ML-Agents offers both. The report tried both and kept PPO: SAC was not training better (§11.2.5).

    §11.1 Agent design

    Inside the learning drone

    Every amber drone runs the same small neural network. Each sees the world only relative to its own nose, picks where to aim, and fires automatically.

    Centralized vs. distributed brain (§11.1.1)

    • First attempt: one brain flies all 10 drones. Too many inputs and outputs; training never converged.
    • Fix: each drone runs its own copy of one shared network (parameter sharing).
      • Small input, 2 outputs, 10× the training samples per frame.
      • A dead drone removes one copy, not the command post.

    Reward structure (§11.1.3)

    editable in §11.2

    Ordering matters. Strickland et al. (§8.4) and this project's early agents learned to flee: when dying to the enemy costs more than hitting a wall, flying into the wall is the "best" policy. So the kill reward (+10) is set to outweigh death (−5), and boundaries cost −50.

    §11.2 Agent training · §11.3–11.5 results · Fig. 11.4 config

    Train the agent yourself

    A complete PPO trainer runs in a background worker: behavioural cloning, GAIL, curiosity, a seven-lesson curriculum and K-frame decisions, configured like the report's Figure 11.4.

    • K-frame updates (§11.2.1): decide every K frames (5 → 4 → 3), hold the aim between.
      • Random early aims need a few frames to have any visible effect; otherwise reward is noise.
    • Imitation (§11.2.2): recorded Select Nearest demonstrations, used two ways.
      • Behavioural cloning, strength 0.5: copy the demonstrator's action in the same state.
      • GAIL, strength 0.75: a discriminator learns to tell agent from expert; fooling it pays.
    • Curiosity (§11.2.3), strength 0.25: reward for surprising its own forward model (ICM).
      • Kill rewards are sparse; curiosity keeps it exploring until it finds them.
    • Curriculum (§11.2.4): arena 100 → 500 m, start 75 → 400 m apart, start rotation 0 → 120°.
      • Analogy: counting before calculus. Promote when mean reward clears the lesson threshold.
    • Opponent: Flocking Slow, a slow enemy that never shoots.

    Evaluation: average enemy kills (Appendix B)

    • What the report's agent did (§11.3): fly at the enemy as a group, kill a few in the first pass, overshoot, then wander "lost and confused" while avoiding walls.
    • Kills vs. Flocking Slow over 100 rounds: 2.58 / 4.25 / 3.07 (easy / medium / hard).
    • Ship defense: 7.53 easy, falling to 0.74 hard; the attackers get past while the defenders are still wandering.
    • This rebuild's reference agent (same trainer, run offline for ~500k decisions) climbs all seven lessons and kills 8–10 Flocking Slow drones per round.
      • Far above the report's numbers: this world is simpler (scaled physics, a harmless slow enemy, no Unity noise).
      • Yet against scripted shooters it still loses most rounds. It learned to aim, not a tactic: the report's central finding, reproduced.
    • Try it:
      • Turn off BC and GAIL: watch it struggle to find the sparse kill reward.
      • Load "Early agent" rewards: the wall becomes the cheapest exit and boundary deaths climb.
      • Tick K = 1: aims change before they matter; learning slows.
      • Switch the opponent to Select Nearest or self-play: the report's proposed next steps.
    §12 Ethical implications · §13 Future work · §14 Conclusion

    Limits, ethics, and what comes next

    • The report's position (§12):
      • Simulation research expands knowledge without fielding weapons; stop unethical uses, not the research.
      • If fielded: keep a human able to shut the swarm down or take control (§12.1).
      • Frames the system as defensive: drones that hunt drones.
      • If the US does not do this research, rivals with looser ethics will, and will write the rules (§12.2).
    • What remains open: armed autonomy, accountability for a learned policy, and safety around non-combatants. The report raises these questions; it does not resolve them.
    • Future work (§13):
      • More scripted tactics, also as better demonstrations to imitate.
      • RL vs. stronger scripted enemies: try opponent "Select Nearest" in §11.2.
      • RL vs. RL self-play, as in OpenAI's hide-and-seek (§8.3): try "Self-play".
    • Bottom line (§14):
      • Scripted counter-swarm tactics already beat larger swarms and protect a ship better than current defenses.
      • RL produced a drone that fights, not yet one that out-thinks the scripts.
